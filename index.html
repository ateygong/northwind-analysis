Question 1

Are we over-reliant on specific suppliers, and which supplier relationships bring in too little revenue to justify them?

Why it matters

Each Northwind product comes from exactly one supplier, so there is no backup source. If a large supplier has a shortage, raises prices or leaves, that revenue is at risk straight away. At the other end, every supplier relationship takes time to manage, so suppliers that sell very little may not be worth keeping.

Takeaways

  • 4 of our 29 suppliers bring in 41% of revenue, and the top two alone bring in about a quarter. The 8 smallest bring in just 7%.
  • Our biggest single exposure is one wine, Côte de Blaye. It brings in 11% of all revenue and comes from just one supplier.
  • Across the whole supplier base, concentration is low (an HHI of 619, below the 1,500 mark usually treated as concentrated). The risk comes from a few large suppliers, not from the base as a whole.

Business Implication

  • Secure our most important active suppliers. Aux joyeux ecclésiastiques (Côte de Blaye) and Gai pâturage (Raclette Courdavault) are our biggest supply risks. Consider longer-term contracts or a second source for similar products.
  • Plan for a revenue gap at Plutzer and Pavlova. Most of Plutzer's revenue and about a third of Pavlova's comes from discontinued products, so their revenue will fall unless replacement products take over.
  • Review the 8 low-contribution suppliers (Svensk Sjöföda, Mayumi's, Nord-Ost-Fisch, PB Knäckebröd, Lyngbysild, Escargots Nouveaux, Zaanse Snoepfabriek, Refrescos Americanas) to see whether consolidating them would save effort.

Question 2

How much of each product is ordered per year, and which products should we re-evaluate selling?

Why it matters

Knowing whether demand is growing tells us how to plan purchasing and stock. Products that barely sell still cost us shelf space and supplier effort, so we need to know which ones to drop and which ones to keep.

Takeaways

  • Demand more than doubled. We went from about 1,600 units a month in 1996 to about 3,800 in early 1998, and monthly revenue tripled.
  • Our 7 slowest products barely matter to sales. Together they make up under 3% of units and under 2% of revenue. Most are low-priced condiments, confections and seafood.
  • They are not all heading the same way. 4 are fading toward zero, while Laughing Lumberjack Lager and Gravad lax are picking up.

Business Implication

  • Plan purchasing and stock for continued growth.
  • Phase out the four fading products (Röd Kaviar, Louisiana Hot Spiced Okra, Chocolade, Genen Shouyu). They bring in very little revenue, so the downside is small.
  • Keep an eye on Laughing Lumberjack Lager and Gravad lax. They are small but growing.
  • Review three fading products together with their suppliers. Röd Kaviar (Svensk Sjöföda), Chocolade (Zaanse Snoepfabriek) and Genen Shouyu (Mayumi's) all come from suppliers flagged as low contribution in Question 1.

Question 3

Are our sales staff focused on our most valuable markets?

Why it matters

Our sales staff can only cover so many customers. If most of our revenue comes from one region, the team should be set up to serve it. If it isn't, we may be missing growth or overloading some staff.

Takeaways

  • Europe is our most valuable region. It brings in 61% of revenue, more than North and South America combined.
  • But most European sales are handled from the US. US-based staff handle about three-quarters of European revenue, while our UK-based staff handle just a quarter.
  • Staff are not organized around markets. The US/UK split is almost the same in every region, and every employee took orders from most of our 21 countries.
  • Within Europe, six small markets are especially valuable. Austria, Ireland, Sweden, Belgium, Denmark and Switzerland have just 11 customers between them but bring in about a quarter of our total revenue. In every one of them, the largest customer makes up more than half of sales, so losing that customer would hurt.

Business Implication

  • Consider organizing sales by region. Depending on capacity and cost, UK-based staff could take ownership of European customers, since they are closest to our biggest market.
  • Treat the six small European markets as key accounts. Start with Ernst Handel (Austria) and Hungry Owl All-Night Grocers (Ireland), which each make up most of their country's revenue.
  • Grow Europe by winning new customers in these high-value markets. This would also make us less reliant on any single customer.

How we analyzed this ↓

How each question was analyzed

Revenue is calculated from each order line as unit price × quantity × (1 − discount). The data covers 6 months of 1996, all of 1997 and the first 4 months of 1998 plus a few days of May, so yearly comparisons use averages per month rather than yearly totals. Monthly averages and charts leave out May 1998, since orders stop on May 6 and a partial month would understate them.

Question 1: Supplier reliance

  • Linked every order line to its product and supplier, and calculated each supplier's share of total revenue.
  • Compared each share to an equal "fair share": with 29 suppliers, that is 1/29 = 3.45% of revenue each.
  • Flagged suppliers at 2× fair share or more (≥ 6.9%) as over-reliant and those at 0.5× or less (≤ 1.7%) as low contribution.
  • For the over-reliant suppliers, found the single product that drives most of their sales and checked whether it is still sold.
  • Calculated the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), a standard measure of concentration, at the supplier level.

Question 2: Demand and the bottom 10%

  • Totalled units and revenue for every product by year. Because 1996 and 1998 are partial years, compared average units per month rather than yearly totals. The full product-by-year table is in the repository.
  • Plotted total units ordered each month. The last month (May 1998) is left out because orders stop on May 6, and a partial month would look like a sudden drop.
  • Ranked active products by total units over the whole period and took the bottom 10% (7 of 69). The 8 discontinued products were left out, since the decision to drop them has already been made.
  • Labelled each slow seller's trend by comparing 1998 to 1997 monthly units: fading if 1998 is below half of 1997, picking up if it is above 1.5×, otherwise steady.
  • For the table, calculated each product's change in average units per month and revenue per month from 1997 to 1998.

Question 3: Sales staff and valuable markets

  • Grouped the 21 customer countries into three regions: Europe, North America (USA, Canada, Mexico) and South America (Brazil, Argentina, Venezuela).
  • Linked each order to the employee who handled it and to that employee's office (5 staff in the US, 4 in the UK), then split each region's revenue by office.
  • To find the "few customers, high value" countries, used a median split: fewer customers than the median country (3) and more revenue per customer than the median ($12,088). For those countries, measured how much revenue comes from the largest customer.
  • Counted how many countries each employee took orders from.

Data and reproducibility

Source: Northwind Database (Customers, Orders, Order Details, Products, Suppliers, Categories, Employees). All numbers, charts and tables on this page are produced by Python scripts in the project repository; run python src/run_analysis.py to recreate them. See the repository README for setup steps.

Limitations: the data has no cost or margin information, no market-size or competitor data, and no record of supplier lead times or rep capacity. Conclusions about profitability, market share or staffing are therefore suggestions, not measured results.
